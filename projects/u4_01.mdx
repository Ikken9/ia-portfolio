Sentiment analysis in financial domains presents unique challenges due to specialized vocabulary and the high stakes
of accurate classification. This project explores the adaptation of transformer models to classify financial news
sentiment, comparing traditional approaches with modern fine-tuning techniques.

## Dataset and Experimental Setup
The Twitter Financial News Sentiment dataset was utilized, containing 9,543 training and 2,388 validation samples across
three sentiment categories: Bearish (0), Bullish (1), and Neutral (2). The dataset exhibited significant class imbalance
with Neutral sentiments dominating (61.8%), reflecting the natural distribution of financial commentary where most
statements are factual rather than opinionated.

**Data Characteristics:**
- **Class Distribution:** Highly skewed toward neutral sentiment
- **Text Length:** Short financial messages typical of Twitter
- **Vocabulary:** Specialized financial terminology and stock tickers
- **Preprocessing:** Minimal cleaning to preserve financial context

## Exploratory Data Analysis
Initial exploration revealed the inherent challenges in financial sentiment classification. The dataset's class
imbalance mirrored real-world financial communication patterns, where most market commentary remains neutral. Text
length analysis showed concise messages averaging 15-20 tokens, typical of financial Twitter discourse.

Dimensionality reduction using UMAP on TF-IDF features demonstrated partial separability between sentiment classes,
with bullish and bearish sentiments showing more overlap than either did with neutral. This visualization confirmed the
intuition that extreme sentiments share more vocabulary with each other than with neutral statements.

## Baseline Model Performance
A traditional TF-IDF with Logistic Regression pipeline established the performance baseline:

**Baseline Results:**
- **Accuracy:** 77.9%
- **Macro F1-score:** 66.2%
- **Class-wise Performance:** Strong neutral detection (94% recall) but poor bearish recognition (36% recall)

The baseline model struggled particularly with minority classes, reflecting the dataset imbalance and the limitations
of bag-of-words approaches in capturing financial context.

## Transformer Fine-tuning Approach
The FinBERT model, pre-trained specifically on financial texts, was selected for fine-tuning. This domain-specific
starting point provided significant advantages over generic language models.

**Training Configuration:**
- **Learning Rate:** 1e-5 with careful warmup
- **Batch Size:** 8 (constrained by GPU memory)
- **Training Epochs:** 5 with early stopping
- **Evaluation Strategy:** Per-epoch validation with F1 optimization

The training process showed consistent improvement across epochs, with both accuracy and F1 scores stabilizing after 3
epochs, indicating effective convergence without overfitting.

## Comparative Performance Analysis
The transformer approach demonstrated substantial improvements over the traditional baseline:

**Performance Comparison:**
- **Accuracy Improvement:** +8.2% (77.9% → 86.1%)
- **F1-score Improvement:** +22.0% (66.2% → 88.2%)
- **Class-wise Balance:** Significantly better minority class recognition

The fine-tuned FinBERT model showed particular strength in distinguishing between bullish and bearish sentiments,
capturing nuanced financial language that the baseline model missed. The transformer's attention mechanisms proved
effective in identifying key sentiment-bearing phrases within financial context.

## Technical Insights and Challenges
**Tokenization Observations:** The BPE tokenizer effectively handled financial terminology, though it occasionally
split stock tickers and financial abbreviations in ways that might obscure meaning. The model learned to reassemble
these fragments through contextual understanding.

**Class Imbalance Handling:** Despite the skewed distribution, the transformer learned effective representations for all
classes, likely due to its pre-training on financial corpora and the use of appropriate evaluation metrics.

**Computational Constraints:** The 8GB GPU memory limitation necessitated conservative batch sizes, but this didn't
prevent the model from achieving strong performance through effective fine-tuning.

## Error Analysis and Limitations
**Persistent Challenges:**
- **Sarcasm and Irony:** Financial Twitter often contains sarcastic commentary that challenges literal interpretation
- **Mixed Sentiments:** Messages containing both positive and negative elements
- **Domain-specific Ambiguity:** Terms like "correction" or "consolidation" having different connotations in financial vs. general contexts

**Failure Modes:**
- Over-reliance on certain trigger words without considering negation
- Difficulty with emerging financial slang and abbreviations
- Sensitivity to message length and formatting variations

## Conclusions and Practical Implications
The successful fine-tuning of FinBERT demonstrates the power of domain-specific pre-training combined with targeted
fine-tuning. The 22% improvement in F1-score over traditional methods underscores the transformer's ability to capture
financial context and nuanced sentiment.

**Key Success Factors:**
- Domain-specific pre-training providing financial language understanding
- Appropriate handling of class imbalance through macro F1 optimization
- Conservative fine-tuning preserving pre-trained knowledge while adapting to specific task

**Practical Applications:**
- Real-time market sentiment monitoring
- Automated financial news categorization
- Trading signal generation from social media sentiment
- Risk assessment through sentiment analysis

The project validates that while traditional methods provide reasonable baselines, transformer-based approaches offer
significant advantages for specialized domains like finance where context and nuance are critical.
